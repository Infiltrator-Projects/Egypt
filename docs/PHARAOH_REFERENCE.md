# Pharaoh / Cleopatra Reference Notes

This document records the parts of *Pharaoh* and *Cleopatra: Queen of the Nile* that are useful as design references for **Egypt**. It is a research record, not a specification to duplicate copyrighted content.

## Why Pharaoh remains a useful reference

The original game tied city growth to visible economic and service networks particularly well. Its strongest systems reinforced one another:

- houses evolved as residents gained access to more goods and services;
- raw materials fed manufacturing chains;
- warehouses and markets made distribution spatial;
- walkers made services visible;
- the Nile and inundation cycle shaped agriculture;
- large monuments consumed sustained labour and resources;
- trade connected maps to resources they could not produce locally;
- scenarios gradually introduced systems across Egyptian history.

The enduring lesson is not any one building or number. It is that **the city itself is the progression system**.

## Production-chain lessons

Classic examples included relationships such as:

- clay → pottery;
- reeds → papyrus;
- barley → beer;
- flax → linen;
- grain production yielding straw that could participate in other chains;
- stone and other construction materials feeding monuments.

For Egypt, preserve the feeling of interconnected production while creating original recipes, timings, balancing and content.

## Housing lessons

A major strength was evolutionary housing: residences improved when the surrounding city could consistently supply increasingly sophisticated needs.

This generated an excellent feedback loop:

**better city systems → better housing → different population/economy → greater city capability**.

Keep the concept. Redesign the details.

## Visible distribution

Markets did not merely confer an abstract coverage bonus. Buyers travelled to sources, returned with goods, and distributors walked residential streets.

This made shortages spatial and legible.

For Egypt, extend this idea so the player can inspect and trace the entire causal chain of a shortage.

## Nile and farming

The original game made the annual Nile cycle an important differentiator from generic city builders. Floodplain fertility and the availability of land changed with inundation, while labour needs varied through the year.

For Egypt, make the cycle more visually and mechanically explicit: visible rising/retreating water, fertility state, changing labour requirements, irrigation and transport consequences.

## Monument construction

One of the original game's strongest fantasies was that monuments required sustained material production, transport and specialist labour rather than appearing instantly.

For Egypt, push this further with visible construction phases, temporary works, material stockpiles, hauling and specialist crews.

## Walker-routing weakness

A major limitation of the classic design was the behaviour of roaming service walkers. Intersections could make coverage unpredictable, encouraging players to construct highly artificial closed road loops and rely heavily on roadblocks.

For Egypt:

- keep visible walkers;
- make them purposeful;
- let them choose destinations based on need and role;
- retain distance and road topology as important constraints;
- offer player routing/district tools without making them mandatory exploits.

## Employment weakness

The classic recruiter mechanic could require worker-seeking walkers to encounter residential buildings even though the relationship between those houses and the resulting labour pool was abstract. This encouraged tiny artificial housing pockets near remote industry.

For Egypt, employment should represent actual commuting, labour availability and remote settlement/transport problems.

## Religion lesson

Gods, temples, shrines, festivals, blessings and punishments strongly reinforced Egyptian identity. However, ratio-driven temple requirements could become repetitive building spam.

For Egypt, religion should have participation, institutions, festivals and social effects that are more meaningful than satisfying a building count.

## Campaign lesson

The original campaign taught mechanics gradually and used different terrain/resources/objectives to make settlements distinct.

For Egypt, retain gradual historical/systemic progression but aim for stronger persistence: earlier settlements may later become suppliers, trade partners, religious centres or strategic assets.

## Cleopatra expansion lesson

The expansion showed the value of scenarios with unusual local purposes and pressures — tomb construction, funerary goods, grave robbery, new industries and tighter objectives.

For Egypt, every major scenario/settlement should have a reason to exist beyond simply reaching generic population/rating thresholds.

## UI / diagnosis lesson

The original used overlays and contextual building information to expose fire, damage, desirability, services, tax and distribution state.

Modern Egypt should make diagnosis much stronger. A player should be able to click a failing house, see exactly what is missing and trace that missing need through market, storage, manufacturing and raw-material supply.

## Reference sources consulted during initial design research

These sources were used to reconstruct broad mechanics and design history. They are references only; implementation must remain original.

- *Pharaoh* original manual (Impressions Games / Sierra, 1999), available via Steam static assets.
- *Pharaoh* / *Cleopatra* general historical and release information on Wikipedia.
- StrategyWiki *Pharaoh* mechanics pages, particularly religion/system references.
- Contemporary and retrospective reviews including GameSpot and long-form city-builder retrospectives.
- Long-running player discussions in the Impressions Games community regarding walker routing, labour access, roadblocks and temple/culture building behaviour.
- *Pharaoh: A New Era* reviews and documentation for comparison with modernised workforce and UI choices.
- Akhenaten open-source project as a technical/reference point for reconstructed original-game behaviour. **Do not copy its code into Egypt without deliberately reviewing licensing and architectural suitability.**

## Core conclusion

The target is not:

> Make Pharaoh again.

The target is:

> Preserve the things Pharaoh understood extraordinarily well — visible logistics, evolving neighbourhoods, Nile agriculture, interconnected production, bustling walkers, historical progression and monumental construction — then redesign those ideas as a modern simulation with its own identity.
